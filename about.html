Bio

Firstly, as you probably already know, my name is Adam Pace. Born and raised in Calgary, AB, I've always been more of a "tech-guru," as some would say.

Growing up, I spent a lot of my time helping friends and family with their technology issues. Ranging anywhere from their cell phones, TVs, TV boxes, internet, computers, gaming systems, you name it.

It wasn't until I moved to Red Deer in my mid-20s that I somehow stumbled upon the opportunity to go back to school on a government grant.

I never anticipated furthering my education after graduating Grade 12, but I immediately grew quite fond of the idea when I realized one of the programs I could attend through the Academy of Learning was Software & Web Development.

I figured, how cool would it be if I could land a career path in the forefront of development and design on the web, or even create interfaces for software?

One thing I've always known, on top of my natural ability to understand technology, is that I love creativity.

Besides working on technology issues, I'm an avid gamer, particularly when it comes to survival games. To no surprise to anyone who knows me, I always seemed to be the "builder" of any bases that ever had to be made.

I always went above and beyond with every one of my creations. "Good" was never enough for me. I had to have something that really popped when you saw it.

Something extraordinary. Something that made people stop for a moment in awe and become inspired by what I'd created.

That's the same type of drive and devotion I eventually envisioned bringing into a career developing frontend architecture on the web, or in software.

I wanted to create things that could leave that same sense of awe with the people experiencing them.

Biggest Obstacle Out Of School

Finishing my diploma was one thing. Figuring out what to do with it afterward was something else entirely.

I had the education. I had the foundation. I had the desire to build things. What I didn't have was a portfolio that could actually demonstrate what I was capable of.

And that became a problem.

When I looked at the projects I had completed during school, I started asking myself a pretty uncomfortable question:

What exactly am I supposed to showcase?

A Tic-Tac-Toe game? A calculator? Projects that demonstrated that I could write code, but didn't really demonstrate the kind of developer I wanted to become?

I didn't want to graduate with a portfolio that simply said, "Here are some things I made."

I wanted something that could say, "Here is what I can do."

That realization completely changed the direction of this portfolio.

Instead of treating the portfolio as a place to display my projects, I started thinking about the portfolio itself as the project.

If I wanted to demonstrate that I understood frontend architecture, interaction design, animation, visual hierarchy, responsive layouts, state management, and the fundamentals of building a website from scratch, why not build something that actually demonstrated all of those things?

That became the foundation for what you're looking at now.

This website isn't simply a container for my work.

It is the work.

Every interaction, animation, transition, layout, visual effect, and system exists for a reason: to demonstrate what I learned, how I think, and what I'm capable of building when I'm given the freedom to create something of my own.

I needed a portfolio.

So I decided to make the portfolio itself worth showcasing.
